What is financial planning, and what does it cover?

Financial planning is a structured way of managing your money to reach your goals. It starts with where you stand today, puts a cost and a date on each goal, and sets out what to invest, insure, repay and restructure. A complete plan covers eight areas, because a decision in one, such as selling property, changes the others.

  • 01

    Goal-based planning

    A cost, a date and a funding plan for each goal, from education abroad to a second home.

  • 02

    Retirement planning

    The corpus you need, and how to draw income from it so it lasts.

  • 03

    Investment planning

    One asset allocation across equity, debt, real estate, gold and alternatives.

  • 04

    Tax planning

    Regime choice, capital gains timing and structuring, done through the year.

  • 05

    Insurance and risk

    Term and health cover sized to your family, with no investment-linked policies you do not need.

  • 06

    Debt management

    Which loans to prepay, which to keep, and whether to borrow against assets rather than sell them.

  • 07

    Emergency planning

    A liquid reserve so a bad month never forces you to sell investments at the wrong time.

  • 08

    Legacy and succession

    A will, correct nominees, and where needed a family trust, so wealth passes without dispute.

Who needs a financial planner in India?

Anyone with more than one goal and more than one kind of asset benefits from a plan. It matters most when your wealth is large, concentrated or complicated: a business, stock options, property, money abroad, or a big one-off event. Those are the situations where unco-ordinated decisions cost the most.

  • Business owners

    Most of your wealth sits in one company. A plan builds wealth outside it and prepares for a sale or succession.

  • Senior professionals with ESOPs

    High salary, vesting stock and bonuses. When to sell, how to diversify, and the tax on each step.

  • NRIs and returning Indians

    Assets in two countries, two tax systems, and a residential status that changes the rules.

  • Families funding education abroad

    Large costs in foreign currency on a fixed date, with remittance rules and TCS to plan around.

  • Those within 10 years of retiring

    The shift from building wealth to living off it, without running out or taking needless risk.

  • After a liquidity event

    An inheritance, property sale or business exit. What to do with a large sum, and what not to rush.

How does financial planning change at each stage of life?

The priorities shift as you move from building wealth to protecting it and then living off it. In your 30s the plan is about saving rate and protection; by your 60s it is about income, tax and succession. A good plan is rewritten for each stage rather than simply extended.

  1. Late 20s to 30s

    Build the base

    • Term and health cover before the first home loan
    • A saving rate, stepped up with every raise
    • Mostly equity, since goals are far away
    • ESOP and bonus decisions made with a plan

  2. 40s

    Peak earning years

    • Children's education, often abroad, gets funded
    • Surcharge and capital gains start to matter
    • Concentration in a business or employer stock
    • A will written, and nominees set on every account

  3. 50s

    Prepare for independence

    • A retirement corpus target and a date
    • Gradual shift from growth to stability
    • Loans cleared before income stops
    • Business succession or exit planned

  4. 60s and beyond

    Live off your wealth

    • A tax-efficient monthly income from the portfolio
    • Health cover that continues for life
    • Gifts to children during your lifetime
    • Wealth transfer made simple for your heirs

The financial planning process, step by step

Financial planning is not a one-time document. It follows six steps, and the last one repeats every year and at every major life event, because markets, tax rules and your family keep changing.

  1. 01

    Define your goals

    What you want, when, and what it costs in today's money: education, homes, retirement, giving, a business.

  2. 02

    Map where you stand

    Income, expenses, every asset and loan, insurance and past tax returns, pulled into one net worth and cash-flow view.

  3. 03

    Find the gaps

    Which goals are short, where risk is concentrated, where cover is missing, and which gap to close first.

  4. 04

    Write the plan

    An asset allocation, a savings and investment schedule, insurance, debt and tax actions, and a legacy checklist.

  5. 05

    Put it into action

    Investments in direct plans, policies, nominations and documents, carried out with you step by step.

  6. 06

    Review and revise

    At least once a year, and whenever something big happens: a marriage, a child, a sale, a move abroad.

What is included in a financial plan?

A written financial plan should let anyone in your family see where you stand, what each goal needs and what to do next. A complete plan has ten parts, from a net worth statement to a dated action list, and ends with the date of its next review.

At the heart of it is an investment policy statement: a short document that records your goals, risk profile, target allocation and rules for rebalancing. It keeps decisions consistent when markets are noisy.

    Financial planning for business owners and promoters

    For most business owners, the business is both the largest asset and the largest risk. The plan's first job is to build wealth that does not depend on the company, so that a bad year, a partner dispute or a failed succession does not decide your family's future.

    • Separate business and family money

      A regular, tax-efficient draw from the business, whether salary, dividend or profit share, invested outside it every year.

    • Reduce personal guarantees

      Know which family assets back business loans, and ring-fence what should never be at risk.

    • Key-person and partner cover

      Insurance and a buy-sell agreement so the business, and your family's share of it, survive if a partner dies or leaves.

    • Borrow against, rather than sell

      For short-term needs, a loan against securities can avoid selling long-held investments and triggering capital gains.

    • Plan the exit early

      Tax on a stake sale, where the proceeds go, and how to invest a large sum without rushing, planned years ahead.

    • Succession, not just a will

      Who runs the business, who owns it, and fairness between children who join it and those who do not.

    Financial planning for NRIs and returning Indians

    NRIs plan across two countries, two currencies and two tax systems. Your residential status decides what India taxes, and it depends on days spent in India and how much you earn here. The plan also has to respect FEMA rules on accounts, investments and moving money out.

    Residential status

    You are resident if you spend 182 days or more in India in a tax year, or 60 days plus 365 days over the previous four years. For Indian citizens visiting India, the 60 days becomes 120 if your Indian income exceeds ₹15 lakh. An Indian citizen with Indian income above ₹15 lakh who is not taxed anywhere else can be treated as resident.

    The right accounts

    NRE and FCNR deposits hold money earned abroad, with tax-free interest and free repatriation. NRO accounts hold Indian income such as rent and dividends, with interest taxed and repatriation up to USD 1 million a financial year after tax.

    Investing from abroad

    Most mutual funds accept NRIs, but some restrict investors in the US and Canada. US persons also face PFIC reporting on Indian funds, which can make direct shares or other routes better.

    Returning to India

    The years after your return can qualify as "resident but not ordinarily resident", when foreign income is largely outside Indian tax. They are the best window to reorganise overseas assets and accounts.

    Estate and succession planning: will, nominees and trusts

    India has no inheritance tax, so succession planning is about making sure the right people receive your wealth quickly and without dispute. A nominee is usually a custodian for your legal heirs, not the final owner, so a clear will matters even when every account has a nominee.

    Common financial planning mistakes HNIs make

    High earners rarely go wrong by saving too little. They go wrong by collecting products instead of following a plan, and by leaving tax, insurance and succession to chance.

    1. 01

      Too many products

      Dozens of funds and policies bought from different sellers, overlapping each other.

    2. 02

      Insurance mixed with investment

      Endowment and money-back plans with low returns and too little cover.

    3. 03

      Idle cash

      Large balances in savings and current accounts, losing value to inflation.

    4. 04

      One big bet

      Most wealth in one company, stock or city's property market.

    5. 05

      Chasing past returns

      Buying last year's winning fund or theme, and selling in a fall.

    6. 06

      Tax as an afterthought

      Gains booked without planning, and exemptions missed until it is too late.

    7. 07

      Relying on employer cover

      Health and life cover that disappears with a job change or retirement.

    8. 08

      No will

      Leaving heirs to prove their claim across banks, funds and property registries.

Accredited investors can agree fees freely. Distributors are paid commission instead, built into regular plans.

    What changed in 2025 and 2026 that affects your plan?

    Several rule changes over the last two years affect how HNI families invest, pay tax and pass on wealth. A plan written before them is likely out of date, especially on nominations, tax and the investment products it uses.

    • Up to 10 nominees on demat and mutual funds

      SEBI's revised nomination rules, in force from 1 March 2025, allow up to 10 nominees per demat account or mutual fund folio, with shares you choose.

    • Up to four nominees on bank accounts

      From 1 November 2025, bank deposits can have up to four nominees, together or one after another. Lockers allow successive nominees.

    • A new Income-tax Act

      The Income-tax Act, 2025 applies from 1 April 2026, with a single "tax year" and renumbered sections. Income up to ₹12 lakh is effectively tax-free under the new regime.

    • Specialised Investment Funds

      A SEBI-regulated category between mutual funds and PMS, from ₹10 lakh, able to use strategies such as long-short equity.

    • Higher adviser fee limit

      SEBI raised the fixed-fee cap for individual clients to ₹1.51 lakh per family a year, and allowed up to a year's fee in advance.

    Financial planning FAQs

    Straight answers to the questions families ask us before they start a plan.

    Last reviewed 28 September 2026 by Mihir Shah, AVP, Financial Planning, Fintoo.

    What is financial planning?

    Financial planning is a structured way of managing your money to reach your goals. It covers assessing where you stand, putting a cost and a date on each goal, and deciding what to invest, insure, repay and restructure to close the gap. A complete plan brings together investments, tax, insurance, debt, retirement and succession, and is reviewed at least once a year.

    Who needs financial planning?

    Anyone with more than one goal and more than one kind of asset benefits from a plan. It matters most for business owners, senior professionals with stock options, NRIs, families funding education abroad, people within ten years of retirement, and anyone who has just received a large sum from an inheritance, property sale or business exit.

    What is included in a financial plan?

    A complete financial plan includes a net worth statement, a cash-flow analysis, a sheet for each goal, your risk profile, an investment policy with a target asset allocation, a retirement projection, an insurance audit, a tax plan, a succession checklist and a dated action calendar. It should also state when it will next be reviewed.

    What is the difference between financial planning and wealth management?

    Financial planning sets the destination and the route: your goals, what they cost, and how cash flow, tax, insurance and succession fit together. Wealth management usually refers to managing the investments that carry out the plan. For HNIs the two work best together, with the plan deciding how the portfolio is built.

    What does a financial advisor do?

    A financial advisor reviews your income, expenses, assets, loans, insurance and goals, then recommends how to invest, protect and structure your money to meet those goals. A SEBI-registered investment adviser does this for a fee, must act in your interest, and cannot also earn commission from selling you the products they recommend.

    How much does a financial planner cost in India?

    For individual and HUF clients, SEBI caps a registered investment adviser's fee at the higher of ₹1.51 lakh per family a year or 2.5% of the assets advised on, and allows up to one year's fee in advance. Accredited investors and non-individual clients can agree fees freely. Fintoo's first consultation is free, and the plan fee is quoted before you commit.

    What is the difference between a SEBI-registered investment adviser and a mutual fund distributor?

    An investment adviser is paid a fee by you and gives advice, usually in low-cost direct plans. A distributor is paid commission by the fund house, built into regular plans, and sells products. SEBI rules separate the two roles. You can check an adviser's registration on SEBI's list of recognised intermediaries.

    What is a certified financial advisor in India?

    In India, anyone who gives personalised investment advice for a fee must be registered with SEBI as an Investment Adviser. This requires NISM investment adviser certification and enrolment with BSE Administration and Supervision Limited (BASL). Titles such as CFP are professional qualifications; SEBI registration is what makes advice legal and regulated.

    Which is better, CFA or CFP?

    They suit different work. The CFA focuses on investment analysis and portfolio management, and is common among fund managers and analysts. The CFP focuses on personal financial planning: goals, tax, insurance, retirement and estate planning. For a family's financial plan, a CFP-style approach fits better, delivered by a SEBI-registered adviser.

    What is an investment policy statement?

    An investment policy statement is a short written document that records your goals, time horizons, risk profile, target asset allocation, the investments you will and will not use, and the rules for rebalancing. It keeps decisions consistent in volatile markets and gives you and your adviser a fixed reference to review against.

    How often should a financial plan be reviewed?

    At least once a year, and immediately after a major life event such as marriage, a child's birth, a job change, a business sale, an inheritance, a move abroad or a death in the family. Changes to tax rules or a large market move are also good reasons to review asset allocation and goal funding.

    How much life insurance do I need?

    A common rule of thumb is term cover of 10 to 15 times your annual income, plus any outstanding loans, minus investments already set aside for your family's goals. Pure term insurance gives the most cover for the premium. Mixing insurance with investment, as endowment and money-back plans do, usually leaves families under-insured.

    Is there an AI financial advisor in India?

    AI tools can analyse your portfolio, track goals and flag risks, but personalised investment advice in India must come from a SEBI-registered adviser. Fintoo combines both: AI does the groundwork on your full financial picture, and a registered advisor makes the recommendations and stays accountable for them.

    How should business owners approach financial planning?

    Start by building wealth outside the business through a regular, tax-efficient draw. Then protect the family from business risk with adequate personal cover, fewer personal guarantees and a buy-sell agreement with partners. Finally, plan the exit or succession years ahead, including the tax on a stake sale and where the proceeds will go.

    Can NRIs get a financial plan in India?

    Yes. An NRI plan covers residential status, the choice between NRE, NRO and FCNR accounts, which Indian investments are open to you given your country of residence, tax in both countries under the tax treaty, repatriation limits, and the timing of any return to India, when RNOR status can help.

    How much emergency fund and insurance does an HNI family need?

    Common rules of thumb are six months of expenses as an emergency reserve, or twelve for business owners; term life cover of ten to fifteen times annual income plus outstanding loans; and your own family health cover with a super top-up, independent of any employer policy. Your plan should set exact amounts for your situation.

    What happens to my investments if I die without a will?

    Nominees can claim the assets, but in most cases they hold them for your legal heirs, who are decided by succession law if there is no will. This can cause delays and disputes. A clear will, and nominees on every account, avoid this. Demat accounts and mutual fund folios now allow up to 10 nominees, and bank accounts up to four.

    Is financial planning only for the wealthy?

    No, but it becomes more valuable as wealth grows, because the cost of mistakes in tax, concentration and succession grows with it. HNIs also have access to more complex products, such as PMS, AIFs and structured products, where independent advice matters most.
