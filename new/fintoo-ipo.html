What is an IPO, and how does it work?

An IPO is the first time a private company offers its shares to the public. The company, or its existing shareholders, sell shares at a price set within a band, and the shares then start trading on the stock exchange. Every IPO must be approved by SEBI and explained in a detailed offer document.

Companies go public to raise money for growth or to repay debt, and to let early investors and founders sell part of their stake. Which of these it is tells you a lot about the issue.

From private company to listed share

  1. 01

    Draft filed

    The company files a DRHP with SEBI for review.

  2. 02

    Price band set

    The final RHP and price band are published.

  3. 03

    You bid

    The issue is open for at least three working days.

  4. 04

    Shares list

    Trading begins on the NSE and BSE.

Six terms worth knowing

Price band
The range within which you can bid. The upper end can be at most 20% above the lower end.
Lot size
The minimum number of shares you must bid for, and the multiple for larger bids.
Cut-off price
An option for retail investors to accept whatever final price is set, so the bid is not rejected.
ASBA and UPI mandate
Your bid amount is blocked in your bank account, not debited, until shares are allotted.
DRHP and RHP
The draft and final offer documents: the business, financials, risks and use of money.
Anchor investors
Large institutions that commit a day before the issue opens, with a lock-in after listing.

Why invest in IPOs, and what are the risks?

An IPO lets you own a company from its first day on the market, at the same price as large investors. But a new listing has no trading history, and many IPOs are priced for the seller, not the buyer. Here is both sides, at a glance.

+Why people invest

  • Early-stage growth

    Get in at listing on companies in expanding markets.

  • Same price for everyone

    Retail investors pay the same final price as institutions.

  • Full disclosure

    The RHP sets out financials, risks and plans in detail.

  • Money stays with you

    ASBA only blocks funds; they keep earning bank interest.

  • Fair chance for small bids

    Oversubscribed retail quotas are allotted by lottery.

  • Quick and paperless

    Apply by UPI in minutes; unallotted money is released automatically.

!Risks to keep in mind

  • Rich pricingSellers choose when to list, often when valuations are high.
  • No track recordThere is no share price history to judge the stock by.
  • Listing can disappointShares can list below the issue price, even after heavy demand.
  • Hype and herd buyingGrey market buzz is unregulated and often wrong.

The fix for most of these: read the offer document, compare the price with listed peers, and apply only to businesses you would hold for years.

What are the different types of IPOs?

IPOs differ in how the price is set, where the shares list and where the money goes. Most large Indian IPOs are book-built mainboard issues, often mixing a fresh issue with an offer for sale. Knowing the type tells you what you are really buying.

Who gets shares in an IPO?

Every IPO splits its shares into investor categories. In a typical issue from a profitable company, at least 35% is kept for retail investors, who bid up to ₹2 lakh. Each category is allotted separately, so heavy demand from institutions does not crowd out small investors.

Up to 60% of the institutional portion can go to anchor investors before the issue opens. Half their shares are locked for 30 days after allotment and half for 90 days. Loss-making companies keep a smaller retail quota of 10%.

How do you apply for an IPO, and what happens after?

You need a PAN, a demat account and a bank account linked to UPI or net banking. Apply during the three-day bidding window; allotment is decided the next working day and shares list three working days after the issue closes.

  1. Day 1 to 3

    Bidding

    Choose lots and price, then approve the UPI mandate in your app.

  2. T

    Issue closes

    UPI bids close at 5 pm on the last day. Your money stays blocked.

  3. T+1

    Allotment

    The registrar finalises who gets shares. Check status on its website.

  4. T+2

    Shares or refund

    Shares reach your demat; unused money is unblocked automatically.

  5. T+3

    Listing day

    Shares start trading on the NSE and BSE from 10 am.

If you get no shares, the block is released by T+2. There is nothing to claim.

Why didn't I get shares in a popular IPO?

When the retail portion is oversubscribed, shares are given one lot each to applicants picked by lottery. If retail is subscribed 50 times, roughly one in 50 applicants gets a lot. Bidding for more lots does not improve your odds.

How are IPO gains taxed in India?

IPO shares are taxed like any other listed shares. Selling within 12 months of allotment, including on listing day, is a short-term gain taxed at 20%. Hold for longer than 12 months and the rate drops to 12.5%, with the first ₹1.25 lakh of long-term gains each year tax-free.

Common IPO mistakes, and what to do instead

Most IPO losses come from applying on hype rather than on the business. A few habits avoid the worst of it.

  • 01

    Trusting the grey market premium

    Instead: the GMP is unofficial and unregulated. Judge the issue from the RHP.

  • 02

    Applying to every IPO

    Instead: apply only to companies you would be happy to hold for years.

  • 03

    Borrowing to apply

    Instead: use only surplus money. A blocked amount can still list at a loss.

  • 04

    Bidding below the cut-off

    Instead: as a retail investor, tick "cut-off" so your bid is valid at the final price.

  • 05

    Multiple bids on one PAN

    Instead: make one application per PAN; duplicates are all rejected.

  • 06

    Forgetting lock-in expiries

    Instead: note when anchor and promoter lock-ins end; extra selling can follow.

IPO FAQs

Clear answers to what investors ask before applying for an IPO.

Last reviewed 1 October 2026 by Mihir Shah, AVP, Financial Planning, Fintoo.

What is an IPO?

An initial public offering (IPO) is when a company sells its shares to the public for the first time and lists them on a stock exchange such as the NSE or BSE. The company may raise new money through a fresh issue, and existing shareholders may sell part of their stake through an offer for sale.

How can I apply for an IPO in India?

You need a PAN, a demat account and a bank account. Apply online through your broker or an investment app such as Fintoo using a UPI mandate, or through your bank's net banking using ASBA. Choose the number of lots and your price, approve the UPI mandate, and the amount is blocked in your bank account until allotment.

What is an IPO price band?

The price band is the range within which investors can bid for shares in a book-built IPO, for example ₹310 to ₹326. The upper end can be at most 20% above the lower end. The final issue price is set within the band based on demand, usually at the upper end when demand is strong.

What is a green shoe option in an IPO?

A green shoe option lets the company issue extra shares, up to 15% of the issue size, to meet excess demand. A stabilising agent can use it to buy shares in the market for up to 30 days after listing if the price falls below the issue price, which helps steady the share price in its early days.

What is the difference between a fixed-price and a book-building IPO?

In a fixed-price IPO, the company announces one price before the issue opens, and demand is known only after it closes. In a book-building IPO, investors bid within a price band, demand is visible every day, and the final price is discovered from the bids. Most large Indian IPOs today are book-built.

What are the benefits of investing in an IPO?

An IPO lets you invest in a company from its first day of listing, at the same price as institutional investors. The offer document gives detailed disclosure, ASBA keeps your money in your own account until allotment, and retail allotment by lottery gives small investors a fair chance. Gains are not guaranteed, and some IPOs list below the issue price.

How is IPO allotment decided for retail investors?

If the retail portion is not fully subscribed, every valid bid gets shares in full. If it is oversubscribed, the registrar runs a computerised lottery, and as many applicants as possible receive one minimum lot each. Applying for more lots does not improve your chances in the retail category.

What is the cut-off price in an IPO?

The cut-off price option lets retail investors agree to buy at whatever final price is set within the band. Your bid is blocked at the upper end of the band, and if the final price is lower, the extra amount is released. Choosing cut-off ensures your bid is not rejected for being too low.

How long does it take for IPO shares to list?

Under SEBI's T+3 timeline, shares list on the stock exchange three working days after the issue closes. Allotment is finalised on T+1, shares are credited and unused money is unblocked on T+2, and trading begins on T+3.

Can I apply for the same IPO more than once?

No. Only one application is allowed per PAN in an IPO category. If you submit multiple applications on the same PAN, all of them can be rejected. Adult family members with their own PAN and demat accounts can apply separately.

What is the maximum amount I can apply for in an IPO?

In the retail category, you can bid for up to ₹2 lakh. Bids above ₹2 lakh fall in the non-institutional (HNI) category. UPI can be used for bids up to ₹5 lakh; larger bids are made through ASBA with your bank's net banking.

What is the grey market premium (GMP) and should I rely on it?

The grey market premium is the price at which IPO shares are informally traded before listing. It is unofficial, unregulated and can change quickly, and it is not a reliable guide to the listing price or the long-term value of the company. Base your decision on the offer document instead.

What is the difference between a DRHP and an RHP?

The draft red herring prospectus (DRHP) is the first offer document a company files with SEBI for review. The red herring prospectus (RHP) is the final version filed before the issue opens, with SEBI's comments addressed. The RHP contains the price band, issue dates, financials, risk factors and how the money will be used.

What is an SME IPO?

An SME IPO is an issue by a small or medium company that lists on a separate platform, such as NSE Emerge or BSE SME. Minimum application sizes are higher, above ₹2 lakh, and trading is thinner than on the main board. SME IPOs carry higher risk and suit experienced investors.

How are IPO listing gains taxed?

IPO shares are taxed like other listed shares. Selling within 12 months of allotment, including on listing day, is a short-term capital gain taxed at 20%. Selling after 12 months is a long-term gain taxed at 12.5% on gains above ₹1.25 lakh a year. Surcharge and cess apply.

Can NRIs apply for IPOs in India?

Yes. NRIs can apply for IPOs through their NRE or NRO accounts linked to a demat account, under the portfolio investment route. Investments from an NRE account are repatriable. Some brokers do not support UPI for NRI bids, so ASBA through the bank may be needed.
