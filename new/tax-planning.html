What is tax planning, and why does it matter more for HNIs?

Tax planning is using the options the law provides, such as deductions, exemptions, rates and timing, to reduce the tax you pay, legally and with full disclosure. It is not tax evasion, which hides income. For HNIs, tax is often the single largest cost in the portfolio, so small decisions compound into large amounts.

  • Surcharge raises the top rate

    Above ₹50 lakh a surcharge applies, rising to 25% above ₹2 crore in the new regime and 37% above ₹5 crore in the old one, before 4% cess.

  • Income arrives from many places

    Salary, bonus, ESOPs, business profits, rent, interest, dividends and capital gains are each taxed differently, at different times.

  • Large one-off events

    A property sale, business exit or ESOP liquidity event can create a tax bill larger than a year's salary unless it is planned in advance.

  • Assets across borders

    Foreign shares, accounts, property and income must be reported correctly. Mistakes can attract penalties far larger than the tax itself.

  • Family and succession

    How assets are held between spouses, children, HUFs and trusts affects tax every year, and what heirs pay when they eventually sell.

Old vs new tax regime: which is better for you?

The new regime is the default and has lower rates, a ₹75,000 standard deduction and a surcharge capped at 25%. The old regime keeps deductions such as 80C, 80D, HRA and home-loan interest. At high incomes the old regime usually wins only with large deductions, and above ₹5 crore the new regime's lower surcharge often decides it. Compare your own numbers below.

How to save capital gains tax legally

For most HNIs, capital gains are where planning pays most. The main levers are timing (holding past the long-term threshold), offsetting gains with losses, using the annual ₹1.25 lakh equity exemption, and reinvesting property gains under Sections 54, 54F and 54EC.

  1. Tax-loss harvesting

    Offset gains with losses you already have

    Selling investments that are below cost books a capital loss that reduces your taxable gains. Short-term losses can offset short- or long-term gains; long-term losses offset only long-term gains. Unused losses carry forward for eight years if you file your return on time.

  2. Gains harvesting

    Use the ₹1.25 lakh exemption every year

    Book long-term equity gains up to ₹1.25 lakh each year, separately for each family member, and reinvest. It raises your cost price and reduces tax when you eventually sell.

  3. Sections 54 and 54F

    Reinvest in a residential house

    Long-term gains from selling a house (Section 54), or from other assets such as shares or land (Section 54F), can be exempt if reinvested in a residential house within the time limits. The exemption is capped at ₹10 crore.

  4. Section 54EC

    Park land or building gains in specified bonds

    Long-term gains from land or buildings are exempt if invested within six months in specified bonds, such as those of NHAI, REC, PFC and IRFC, up to ₹50 lakh a year, held for five years.

What changed for taxpayers in 2026?

The Income-tax Act, 2025 replaced the 1961 Act from 1 April 2026, and Budget 2026 made targeted changes. Tax rates and slabs stayed the same, but several rules that matter to HNIs moved.

  • A new Income-tax Act and "tax year"

    "Previous year" and "assessment year" are replaced by a single tax year, and most sections are renumbered, for example 80C is now Section 123. Rates and deductions carried over.

  • Share buybacks taxed as capital gains

    From 1 April 2026, buyback proceeds are taxed as capital gains rather than as dividends, with a higher effective rate for promoters.

  • More time to revise a return

    Revised returns can now be filed until 31 March, with an additional fee after 31 December. ITR-3 and ITR-4 are due on 31 August.

  • Sovereign gold bond exemption narrowed

    The capital gains exemption on redemption now applies only to bonds bought at the original issue, not those bought on the exchange.

  • No interest deduction against dividends

    Interest paid on money borrowed to buy dividend-paying investments can no longer be deducted from dividend income.

Tax planning FAQs

Straight answers to the tax questions HNIs, business owners and NRIs ask us most.

What is tax planning?

Tax planning is arranging your income, investments and spending so you pay the least tax the law allows. It covers choosing between the old and new regimes, claiming the deductions and exemptions you are entitled to, timing the sale of assets, and structuring business, property and family income. Done through the year, it saves far more than a last-minute scramble in March.

Is tax planning legal? What is the difference between tax avoidance and tax evasion?

Tax planning is legal. It means using deductions, exemptions and choices the Income-tax Act offers. Tax evasion, such as hiding income or inflating expenses, is a crime. Aggressive avoidance sits in between: arrangements with no real purpose other than saving tax can be struck down under the general anti-avoidance rules (GAAR). Good planning has commercial substance and full disclosure.

Which is better for HNIs, the old or the new tax regime?

It depends on your deductions. The new regime has lower slab rates, a ₹75,000 standard deduction and a surcharge capped at 25%, but allows few deductions. The old regime can win when HRA, home-loan interest, 80C, 80D and NPS together are large. Above ₹5 crore, the new regime usually wins because the old regime's 37% surcharge applies. Compare both every year.

How can HNIs legally reduce income tax in India?

The biggest savings for HNIs rarely come from 80C. They come from choosing the right regime, harvesting capital gains and losses each year, reinvesting property gains under Sections 54, 54F or 54EC, using employer NPS contributions, structuring business income and family holdings sensibly, planning buybacks and dividends, and claiming relief for tax paid abroad. Each should be modelled on your actual numbers.

What is the surcharge on income tax for high earners?

Surcharge is an extra tax on your income tax: 10% above ₹50 lakh, 15% above ₹1 crore, 25% above ₹2 crore, and 37% above ₹5 crore under the old regime only. The new regime caps it at 25%. On dividends and on listed capital gains it is capped at 15%. Marginal relief stops the surcharge from exceeding the extra income, and 4% cess applies on top.

How does tax-loss harvesting work in India?

You sell investments that are below cost to book a capital loss, then use it to offset capital gains in the same year. Short-term losses can offset both short-term and long-term gains; long-term losses can offset only long-term gains. Unused losses carry forward for eight years if you file your return on time. You can also book up to ₹1.25 lakh of long-term equity gains a year tax-free.

How can I save capital gains tax on selling property?

Long-term gains on a house can be exempted by buying or building another residential house in India under Section 54, or, for other long-term assets, by investing the net sale proceeds under Section 54F; both exemptions are capped at ₹10 crore. Or invest up to ₹50 lakh within six months in 54EC bonds from NHAI, REC, PFC or IRFC, locked in for five years.

Is there inheritance tax in India?

No. India abolished estate duty in 1985 and has no inheritance tax today. Assets you inherit are not taxed when received, but income from them, and any gain when you sell them, is taxed in your hands, using the original owner's cost and holding period. Assets abroad may attract inheritance or estate tax in that country, so cross-border families need planning.

How are share buybacks taxed from April 2026?

From 1 April 2026, money received in a share buyback is taxed as a capital gain in the shareholder's hands, rather than as a dividend as it was from October 2024. Your cost is deducted and the gain is taxed at short-term or long-term rates depending on how long you held the shares. Promoters face a higher effective rate. Check the current rules before tendering shares.

What are the advance tax due dates?

If your tax after TDS will be ₹10,000 or more in a year, you must pay advance tax in instalments: at least 15% by 15 June, 45% by 15 September, 75% by 15 December and 100% by 15 March. Missing an instalment attracts interest. Resident senior citizens with no business income are exempt.

How are NRIs taxed in India?

NRIs pay Indian tax only on income earned or received in India, such as rent, interest, dividends and capital gains on Indian assets. NRE deposit interest is tax-free; NRO interest is taxable, with TDS usually at higher rates. A tax treaty (DTAA) with your country of residence can lower the rate or give credit for tax paid. Residential status depends on days spent in India.

What changed with the Income-tax Act, 2025?

The new Income-tax Act, 2025 replaced the 1961 Act from 1 April 2026. It uses a single 'tax year' instead of previous and assessment years, renumbers sections (80C, for example, is now Section 123), and simplifies the language. Slab rates did not change. Budget 2026 also changed buyback taxation, removed interest deductions against dividends, and extended the revised-return deadline to 31 March.
